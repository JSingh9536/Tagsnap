01 Where the money goes

Every ticket is typed by hand, and nobody can prove what it said.

Tickets ride around in a truck for a week, land in an envelope, and somebody keys them into a spreadsheet. It works, right up until it doesn’t.

  • 01
    The same ticket gets paid twice.

    Nothing checks. It is the most common way money leaks out of a haul operation.

  • 02
    A digit gets transposed.

    22.32 tons becomes 223.2, or 2.232. Nobody notices until the quarry’s invoice arrives.

  • 03
    A driver disputes their settlement.

    You have a spreadsheet cell. They have a memory. The paper is gone.

  • 04
    Subhauler pay and driver pay get tangled.

    A vendor payable ends up in a payroll run, or the reverse.

The hidden cost

At 500 tickets a month and two minutes each, keying them by hand is roughly 17 hours — every month, forever, and the errors survive it.

What changes

The photo becomes the evidence, permanently. Every number that changes is attached to a person and a timestamp. A dispute is answered in ten seconds by pulling the original ticket up next to who touched it.

02 How it works

Photograph it once. Everything after that is checking, not typing.

The old photo is kept

The new one replaces it on screen but never destroys it. The first photo is the evidence for whatever was already read off it.

Still one ticket

A retake attaches to the same ticket rather than creating a second one — which is exactly the duplicate the system exists to prevent.

No signal, no problem

Photos save to the phone the instant they are taken and send themselves later. A quarry in a dead zone never blocks a driver.

04 Two books

Your drivers and your subhaulers never end up on the same invoice.

The app asks which one you are before it asks anything else, and the answer follows the ticket all the way to the payment.

Company drivers

Your employee, your truck. Their loads land on a settlement, priced off your driver rate schedule.

Subhaulers

An outside outfit. You owe the outfit, once, for what its trucks moved — no matter which of their drivers was behind the wheel that day.

Nobody sees the other

Two subhaulers can never see each other’s tonnage or rates. That is competitive information, and it is walled off structurally.

05 The controls

Nine checks run on every ticket before anyone can approve it.

These are not warnings in a screen someone can click past. They are rules in the database, which means they hold even against someone deliberately working around the app.

Check What it catches Status
Same ticket number, same quarry The same ticket submitted twice — the most common way money leaks Built
Image fingerprint The same paper ticket re-photographed at a different angle with the number changed Built
Gross − tare = net A misread or altered digit. Three numbers checking each other, free Built
Net vs the truck’s legal capacity Impossible loads, and 223.2 typed where 22.32 belongs Built
Tare vs that truck’s usual tare A misread empty weight — tare barely changes, so it verifies itself Built
Date inside the pay window Backdating, and old tickets resurfacing after a period was paid Built
A rate exists for this load Silently paying zero, or paying last year’s rate Built
Submitter cannot approve A driver approving their own pay. Enforced as a hard rule, not a convention Built
Approved tickets are frozen Quietly editing a paid amount after the fact. Nobody can — including admins Built
Match against the quarry’s own invoice Everything above, proven against the counterparty’s records Not yet
The last one is the prize.

The quarry already sends you a monthly invoice listing every ticket number and tonnage. Match your tickets against it and anything that reconciles is proven correct without a person ever looking. Only the mismatches get attention — and those need attention anyway, because a mismatch means either a misread or the quarry billed you wrong. Either way it is money.

06 Options

Four ways tickets can reach the system. Photos are the fallback, not the goal.

They all feed the same pipeline, so you can mix them — and add a better one later without rebuilding anything.

B  Office desk scanner A document scanner with a feeder. Drivers drop tickets in an envelope; the office feeds the stack ~$400 once One tap

Do option A first. It is free and it might delete the problem.

Call your top five quarries and ask: “Do you have e-ticketing, or can you send me a daily file of my tickets? CSV, Excel, PDF, emailed, portal export — whatever you already produce.” Ask about Command Alkon, HaulHub, or Trux by name. One yes from a high-volume quarry removes hundreds of photos a month permanently.

What it replaces

At 500 tickets a month and two minutes each, hand-keying is about 17 hours a month. At a loaded $22 an hour that is roughly $370 a month of somebody’s time — against $40 of running cost.

The typing goes to zero. The checking does not, and should not — but checking a pre-filled ticket is seconds, not minutes.

And the thing you can’t price

One duplicate ticket caught, or one settlement dispute answered from the original photo instead of an argument, covers a year of this. That is not a projection — it is what the checks are for.

Estimates, not quotes. Ticket reading is metered by the provider and the per-ticket figure depends on photo size; the rest are published list prices as of writing. Nothing here has been billed yet.

09 What happens next

Six things, and four of them are free.

  • 01
    Make the five phone calls.

    Ask your biggest quarries for a digital ticket file. Costs nothing, takes an afternoon, and may remove entire quarries from this system permanently.

  • 02
    Collect 20–50 real tickets.

    Deliberately including the faded, greasy, folded ones. Run them through and count how often the net tonnage comes out right. That one number decides how heavy the review step needs to be.

  • 03
    Open an Anthropic account and put $20 on it.

    That is roughly a thousand tickets — far more than you need to decide whether this works. Set a $50 monthly spend limit while you are in there.

  • 04
    Answer four questions.

    How driver pay is actually calculated. What a pay period is. Who approves. Whether subhaulers are paid on the same basis as your drivers. The system runs without these; it cannot go live without them.

  • 05
    Run it on one week of real tickets.

    Alongside the current process, not instead of it. Compare the two settlements at the end of the week. If they match, you are done deciding.

  • 06
    Then buy the store accounts.

    $99 to Apple and $25 to Google, and only once the week above went well. Everything before this point is free or nearly so.

Still to build

Connecting your Google Sheets rate table and invoice export — about a day’s work, but it needs somebody to see the real sheet first. A screen for editing rates. And matching against the quarry’s monthly invoice, which is the piece that eventually retires most of the reviewing.
